Sir Edward Don of Horsenden, a few miles from Ilmer, kept a household book from 1510 until his death in 1551. Between the payments for ale, strawberries and shaving, he noted his neighbours. The vicar of Ilmer appears in three entries, and the village in three more.

For the whole of this period the vicar was [James Withington](/wills/james-withington), appointed in 1516 and buried in Ilmer church forty years later. Sir Edward never names him. He is always "the vycary of Ylmer".

### The hidden money, May 1533

On a page headed with the name W. Wallerewne and the words "the vycar of Bledlowe", Sir Edward set down what had been found hidden in a house. A few lines below he notes that the vicar of Bledlow had died a few days before Christmas 1531, so the money appears to have been his.

| Where it was hidden            | What was there               | What became of it                                                    |
| :----------------------------- | :--------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| In one hiding place            | £8 in gold                   | Found whole and delivered to the vicar of Ilmer                      |
| In a corner, under a threshold | £16 in silver                | Found by "sertayne women of hys kyn", who handed the vicar £12 of it |
| In a hole in the wall          | £28 in gold angels and ryals | Stolen before he died, and kept quiet until a little before Easter   |

The stolen gold came to light "by confesshyun". It too reached the vicar of Ilmer, who gave the thief 5 marks of silver (£3 6s 8d) and then divided the gold between himself and the commissary at the King's Head in Aylesbury.

A further 8 marks went to four men to pay a priest to sing for the dead man's soul for a year.

Sir Edward does not say why the vicar of Ilmer was the man everyone brought the money to. The commissary was the church officer who dealt with the estates of the dead, so James Withington was probably acting as executor or administrator for his neighbour. One is left to wonder what the women did with the other £4.

source: _[The Household Book of Sir Edward Don](https://bucksrecsoc.org.uk/BRS-VOLUMES/brs-vol-33.pdf#page=304), f.209, Bucks Record Society vol. 33_

The Tryp family were Ilmer people. A William Tripp was churchwarden in 1483.

After Sir Edward's death his Buckinghamshire estate was divided between his granddaughters. The lands in Ilmer went to Frances and her husband Ralph Lee, under an agreement completed in August 1562.
